\section{Solving the allocation, and certifying it}
\label{sec:algorithm}

Problem~\eqref{eq:allocation} is solved twice: once from above, by a descent
that returns an attainable schedule, and once from below, by a relaxation that
bounds what any schedule could achieve. The pair is what licenses the language
used for the result.

\subsection{Attainable schedule: budgeted coordinate descent}
\label{sec:algorithm-descent}

Introduce the multiplier $\lambda$ on the budget and minimize
$J(\mathbf u)+\lambda\sum_q W_q N_q^2$ by sweeping the \emph{decision
intervals}. Holding all other intervals fixed, the terms involving $N_q$ are

\begin{equation}
  N_q \;\leftarrow\; \argmin_{N\in\mathcal{N}}\;
  \Bigl[\;
    \underbrace{\sum_{i,k\in I_q}\mathbf u_i(N)^{\top}\mathbf Q_{ik}\,
      \mathbf u_k(N)}_{\text{within block, Eq.~\eqref{eq:within-block}}}
    \;+\; 2\sum_{i\in I_q}\mathbf u_i(N)^{\top}\mathbf c_i^{(-q)}
    \;+\; \lambda\,W_q\,N^2
  \;\Bigr] ,
  \label{eq:coordinate-update}
\end{equation}

with the interval time weight $W_q$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:interval-weight} in place
of the sample count, and
$\mathbf c_i^{(-q)}=\mathbf c_i-\sum_{k\in I_q}\mathbf Q_{ik}\mathbf u_k$
from Eq.~\eqref{eq:cross-term}. The first group of terms is the block's own
contribution --- a magnitude, which is what a separable method would see ---
and the second is the coupling: it rewards a defect pointing \emph{against} the
accumulated displacement. That second term is the whole difference between this
and a sensitivity-weighted allocation.

\begin{algorithm}[!htbp]
\caption{Budgeted coordinate descent for Eq.~\eqref{eq:allocation}}
\label{alg:descent}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require tabulated $\mathbf u_i(N)$ for $i=1..M$, $N\in\mathcal{N}$; suffix
  blocks $\mathbf A_i$; grouping $g$; budget $B$; start set $\mathcal{S}$
  (Table~\ref{tab:starts})
\State solve at $\lambda=0$; \textbf{if} $W(N^\star)\le B$ \textbf{return}
  \Comment{$\lambda^\star=0$: the ceiling does not bind}
\State $\lambda_{\mathrm{hi}} \gets$ the separable solution's multiplier;
  double it until $W\le B$ \Comment{bracket $[0,\lambda_{\mathrm{hi}}]$}
\Repeat \Comment{outer: bisection inside the bracket}
  \For{each start $s\in\mathcal{S}$} \Comment{multi-start, \emph{not} one start}
    \State $N \gets s$
    \Repeat \Comment{inner: sweeps}
      \State build the suffix array $\sum_{k>i}\mathbf A_k\mathbf u_k$ once,
        from the right \Comment{frozen for this sweep}
      \For{$q = 1 \dots K_{\mathrm{dec}}$ \textbf{in temporal order}}
        \State update $N_q$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:coordinate-update}; carry the
          change forward as a running prefix offset
      \EndFor
    \Until{no interval changes, or sweep limit}
    \State record the triple $\bigl(J_s,\;W_s,\;
      F_\lambda^{(s)}=J_s+\lambda W_s\bigr)$
  \EndFor
  \State $N^\star(\lambda) \gets \argmin_s F_\lambda^{(s)}$
    \Comment{by the \emph{penalized} objective, not by $J$}
  \State \textbf{if} $W(N^\star)\le B$ \textbf{then} keep $N^\star$ among the
    feasible candidates \Comment{best $J$ wins, whichever $\lambda$ found it}
  \State $\lambda \gets$ bisect within $[0,\lambda_{\mathrm{hi}}]$
    \Comment{search accelerator only}
\Until{bracket exhausted}
\State $N^{\dagger} \gets$ the feasible candidate of smallest $J$
\State $N^{\dagger} \gets \textsc{Polish}(N^{\dagger},B)$
  \Comment{Alg.~\ref{alg:polish}: search under the constraint itself}
\State \Return $N^{\dagger}$, $J(N^{\dagger})$, and the spread of $J$ across
  $\mathcal{S}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{The budget is a ceiling, and the optimum need not reach it.}
Problem~\eqref{eq:allocation} constrains $\sum_qW_qN_q^2\le B$, and a schedule
that leaves work unspent is admissible. That is not a corner case to be tidied
away. The objective is \emph{not} monotone in degree --- the whole content of
the signed formulation is that contributions cancel, so raising a degree can
shrink one term of a cancelling pair and \emph{increase} $J$. An allocator that
spends its last unit of work can therefore be worse than one that stops short,
and Algorithm~\ref{alg:descent} tests $\lambda=0$ before it brackets anything.

\paragraph{The multiplier is a search device, not an optimality condition.}
The degrees are integers, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:allocation} is an integer program
and the Karush--Kuhn--Tucker conditions are not necessary for its optimum.
Complementary slackness fails outright. Two candidates with $(W,J)$ of $(5,1)$
and $(10,0)$ under $B=6$ have the first as their constrained optimum: it
spends five of six, and there is no $\lambda\ge0$ for which
$\lambda\,(B-W^{\star})=0$ while $N^{\star}$ minimises $J+\lambda W$. A sweep
stopped on $\lambda\,(B-W)<\epsilon$ would reject the right answer.

The limitation is more general than that example. Minimising $J+\lambda W$ is
a weighted-sum scalarization, and over a discrete set such a scalarization
reaches only the \emph{supported} points of the trade-off curve --- the same
duality gap Section~\ref{sec:related-allocation} notes for separable discrete
allocation. A constrained optimum lying in a non-convex notch is unreachable
from every multiplier, however finely the sweep is refined.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:descent} is therefore stated as what it is: a Lagrangian
continuation that generates good feasible schedules cheaply. It keeps the
smallest-$J$ schedule among \emph{all} feasible ones the sweep produced rather
than whichever the last bracket happened to land on --- returning the last
lets a larger start set come back \emph{worse} than a smaller one, which we
observed before fixing it. The result is then improved under the constraint as
written, with no penalty term:

\begin{equation}
  N_q \;\gets\; \argmin_{N\;:\;W_{-q}+W_qN^2\le B} J ,
  \label{eq:feasible-polish}
\end{equation}

together with a paired exchange $(N_p,N_q)\to(N_p',N_q')$ subject to $W\le B$,
which supplies the direction Eq.~\eqref{eq:feasible-polish} lacks: a raise
blocked by the ceiling becomes affordable only if something else comes down.
Pairs are proposed by marginal rate and accepted on an exact re-evaluation,
since two intervals' cross terms see each other and the sum of two
independently computed changes is not the change of applying both.

\begin{algorithm}[!htbp]
\caption{\textsc{Polish}: search under the ceiling, with no penalty term}
\label{alg:polish}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require feasible $N$; budget $B$; tabulated $\mathbf u_i(N)$
\Repeat
  \State $\Delta J_{qN},\ \Delta W_{qN} \gets$ exact change from moving
    interval $q$ to $N$, all $q$ and $N$ \Comment{one pass, same algebra as
    the sweep at $\lambda=0$}
  \If{some $\Delta J_{qN}<0$ with $\Delta W_{qN}\le B-W$}
    \State apply the most negative such move
      \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:feasible-polish}}
  \Else
    \State rank raises by $\Delta J/\Delta W$ and lowers by
      $\Delta J/(-\Delta W)$; for the leading few pairs $(p,q)$, $p\ne q$:
    \State \quad apply both, \textbf{re-evaluate $J$ exactly}, keep the first
      that fits and improves \Comment{the ranking is a proposal; the
      acceptance is not}
  \EndIf
\Until{nothing moves}
\State \Return $N$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

No step of this claims optimality. What measures the distance to it is the
enumeration of Section~\ref{sec:algorithm-exhaustive}, and on unstructured
test instances the pipeline is demonstrably \emph{not} optimal --- worst case
\ph{value} above the enumerated optimum --- which is reported rather than
bounded.

Two consequences run through the rest of the paper. Comparisons are matched on
a common realized-work \emph{ceiling} rather than on equal spend, and each
policy's realized work is reported beside its error, so that a policy winning
while spending less is read as winning by more. And if the benchmark
systematically declines work at high budgets, that is a statement about the
physics --- additional fidelity destroying cancellation --- to be reported
rather than hidden by writing the constraint as an equality.

\paragraph{Which start wins is decided by the penalized objective.} At a fixed
$\lambda$ the starts land in different basins, and those basins need not spend
the same work: $J_s$ and $W_s$ both vary across $\mathcal S$. Selecting on
$J_s$ alone would therefore prefer whichever basin bought a lower objective by
spending more, which is not the solution of the Lagrangian subproblem and
would leave the attained work $W(\lambda)$ free to rise with $\lambda$ for
reasons that have nothing to do with the problem --- undermining the very
monotonicity the outer bisection needs. The selection is on
$F_\lambda=J+\lambda W$; all three quantities are recorded, and once the
budget is matched the policies are compared on $J$.

The multi-start loop sits \emph{inside} the bisection rather than outside it:
each $\lambda$ is solved from scratch from all starts. That is more expensive
than warm-starting from the previous $\lambda$, and it is deliberate --- a
warm start would make the returned schedule depend on the bisection path, which
is precisely the failure mode the monotonicity check below is meant to detect.

Each sweep is $\mathcal{O}(M\lvert\mathcal{N}\rvert)$ by
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:cross-term} and~\eqref{eq:within-block} --- grouping changes the
number of decision variables but not the sweep cost --- and no field evaluation
occurs inside the loop: the $\mathbf u_i(N)$ table is built once per orbit.

That bound needs one asymmetry to hold, and it is easy to implement the wrong
way. Updating interval $q$ changes $\mathbf u$ on $I_q$, which changes the
prefix sum at every later cell and the suffix sum at every earlier one.
Rewriting either array would cost $\mathcal{O}(M)$ per interval and
$\mathcal{O}(MK_{\mathrm{dec}})$ per sweep. Neither is necessary. Sweeping in
temporal order, the prefix change is a single constant carried forward as a
running offset, and the suffix is only ever read to the right of $I_q$ --- a
region this sweep has not touched --- so the array built at the start of the
sweep stays valid throughout it. The stale suffix is not an approximation but
the definition: it is what makes the update Gauss--Seidel in the intervals and
Jacobi across the sweep.

\paragraph{Summation.} The prefix sums are accumulated in compensated
(Neumaier) arithmetic. This is not routine caution: $\mathbf S_j$ is a sum of
order $10^5$ signed terms whose cancellation is the very quantity the method
exploits, so the summation is ill-conditioned by construction, and the ratio of
$\sum_i\lVert\mathbf u_i\rVert$ to $\lVert\mathbf S_M\rVert$ is reported per
orbit as the conditioning diagnostic. \wpref{WP1}

The objective is not convex in the integer variables, so the descent is
started from several points and the spread across starts is reported rather
than suppressed \wpref{WP3}.

\paragraph{The outer bisection needs a monotonicity check.} For a global
minimizer the attained work $W(\lambda)$ is non-increasing in $\lambda$, which
is what licenses bisecting on it. What Algorithm~\ref{alg:descent} returns is a
best-of-starts local solution, and nothing guarantees that the basin it lands
in varies monotonically. We therefore sweep $\lambda$ on a dense logarithmic
grid before any campaign run and verify that the best-of-starts $W(\lambda)$ is
non-increasing. Where it is, bisection is used. Where it is not, the bisection
is abandoned for that orbit and the schedule is chosen as the feasible grid
point with the smallest $J$, which is a weaker procedure but not an unsound
one; the orbits on which this happens are counted and reported. \wpref{WP3}

\subsection{Certificate: convex-hull relaxation and a Frank--Wolfe gap}
\label{sec:algorithm-certificate}

Calling the descent result a benchmark requires knowing how far it can be from
the best possible schedule. Relax each \emph{decision interval} to the convex
hull of its candidate contributions,

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf u_i \;=\; \sum_{N\in\mathcal{N}}\theta_{g(i)N}\,\mathbf u_i(N),
  \qquad
  \theta_{qN}\ge0, \quad \sum_N \theta_{qN}=1,
  \qquad
  \sum_q W_q \sum_N \theta_{qN}N^2 \;\le\; B .
  \label{eq:relaxation}
\end{equation}

Every integer schedule is feasible here with $\theta$ an indicator and attains
the same objective, so the relaxed optimum $J^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}$ is a lower
bound on the integer optimum $J^{*}$. The relaxed problem minimizes the convex
quadratic $J$ over a convex set, and its linear subproblem

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf s \;\in\; \argmin_{\theta \text{ feasible}}\;
  \langle \nabla J(\mathbf u), \mathbf u(\theta)\rangle
  \label{eq:fw-subproblem}
\end{equation}

decouples across decision intervals under a single multiplier on the budget. It
therefore has the same epoch-separable, single-multiplier \emph{structure} as
the sensitivity-weighted allocation of Section~\ref{sec:benchmark-sens}, but it
is not the same problem: its per-interval objective is
$\langle\nabla_q J,\mathbf u_q(N)\rangle$ rather than
$\sum_{i\in I_q}\Delta t_i\Delta\mathbf a_i^{\top}\mathbf K_i\Delta\mathbf a_i$,
and
$\nabla J = 2\mathbf Q\mathbf u$ depends on the current relaxed iterate.
Frank--Wolfe therefore applies with an
$\mathcal{O}(M\lvert\mathcal{N}\rvert)$ iteration
\citep{frank1956algorithm,jaggi2013revisiting}, and its duality gap supplies
the bound directly:

\begin{equation}
  J(\mathbf u) + \langle\nabla J(\mathbf u),\,\mathbf s-\mathbf u\rangle
  \;\le\; J^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}} \;\le\; J^{*}
  \;\le\; J(\mathbf u_{\mathrm{descent}}) .
  \label{eq:certificate}
\end{equation}

Write $L_{\mathrm{FW}}$ for the best (largest) value the left-hand side attains
over the iteration budget and $J_{\mathrm{desc}}$ for the descent objective.
Because the objective is a squared error, a gap in $J$ and a gap in $E$ are not
the same number, so both are reported:

\begin{equation}
  g_J \;=\; \frac{J_{\mathrm{desc}}-L_{\mathrm{FW}}}{J_{\mathrm{desc}}},
  \qquad
  g_E^{\star} \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{J_{\mathrm{desc}}}{L_{\mathrm{FW}}}}-1 .
  \label{eq:certified-gap}
\end{equation}

The excess is written as a fraction of the \emph{optimum} rather than of the
attained error, and the distinction is not pedantry. From
$E^{\star}\ge\sqrt{L_{\mathrm{FW}}}$ the bound is

\begin{equation}
  \frac{E_{\mathrm{desc}}-E^{\star}}{E^{\star}} \;\le\; g_E^{\star} ,
  \label{eq:relative-excess}
\end{equation}

so $g_E^{\star}$ is exactly the quantity a reader takes it for: the descent
schedule's position error is at most $g_E^{\star}$ above the best any schedule
of this class achieves. The complementary form
$1-\sqrt{L_{\mathrm{FW}}/J_{\mathrm{desc}}}$ measures the same bound against
$E_{\mathrm{desc}}$ instead, and reads about a tenth smaller near the values of
interest --- $0.10$ there is $0.111$ here --- which is enough to matter in a
statement whose whole content is a bound.

Both are reported for every orbit and neither carries a threshold; what the
naming rule is bound to is the exhaustive panel of
Section~\ref{sec:algorithm-exhaustive}.

Two degenerate cases are handled by rule rather than by judgement. The
Frank--Wolfe bound is valid but can be vacuous early in the iteration, where
the left-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:certificate} may fall below zero; since
$J\ge0$ always, $L_{\mathrm{FW}}$ is taken as
$\max\{0,\ \text{best bound}\}$. If $L_{\mathrm{FW}}=0$ when the iteration
budget is exhausted, the certificate is vacuous, no gap is reported for that
orbit, and the orbit is counted in a separate column rather than dropped.

\paragraph{The subproblem must be solved exactly.} The bound in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:certificate} is valid only if $\mathbf s$ attains the minimum of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fw-subproblem} over the \emph{relaxed} feasible set. Stopping the
multiplier bisection at the nearest schedule whose integer budget falls below
$B$ does not do that: it returns a feasible point, not a minimizer, and the
resulting quantity is no longer a lower bound.

It is worth being precise about which pair of degrees a fractional solution
mixes, because the natural guess is wrong. Each candidate contributes a point
$\bigl(c_N,\ell_N\bigr)=\bigl(N^2,\ \langle\nabla_q J,\mathbf u_q(N)\rangle
\bigr)$ in a cost--value plane, and the multiplier solution of the relaxed
subproblem selects vertices of the \emph{lower convex envelope} of those
points. The pair mixed at the critical multiplier is a pair of neighbouring
vertices \emph{of that envelope}, which need not be neighbours in
$\mathcal{N}$: a candidate lying above the envelope --- say $N=50$ when
$\mathcal{N}=\{40,50,60\}$ --- is never selected at any multiplier, and the
exact solution mixes $40$ with $60$. Several intervals can also tie at the
same critical multiplier, and the saturating weights must then be apportioned
across all of them.

Rather than implement that reasoning, the first version solves
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fw-subproblem} as what it is --- a small linear program over the
product of simplices with one knapsack row --- with
\code{scipy.optimize.linprog} \citep{virtanen2020scipy}. It is
$K_{\mathrm{dec}}\lvert\mathcal{N}\rvert$ variables and
$K_{\mathrm{dec}}{+}1$ constraints, which is unremarkable for a simplex or
interior-point solver, and it is exact by construction rather than by an
argument about envelopes. A hand-rolled multiplier solver may replace it if
profiling demands one, and Section~\ref{sec:ablations} checks the two against
a brute-force optimum on short instances either way. The certificate is one of
the paper's stronger claims; a dull verified solver is worth more here than a
clever fragile one.

The subproblem is solved over the feasible set as written,
$\sum_qW_q\sum_N\theta_{qN}N^2\le B$, and \emph{not} over its boundary. That
distinction is about validity, not tidiness. If the programme's optimum leaves
slack and the implementation pushes it onto the budget surface regardless,
$\mathbf s$ stops being a minimizer of Eq.~\eqref{eq:fw-subproblem} and the
left-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:certificate} stops being a lower bound. The
slack case is not hypothetical here: $\langle\nabla_qJ,\mathbf u_q(N)\rangle$
is no more monotone in $N$ than $J$ is, for the same cancellation reason.
What the implementation asserts on every iteration is therefore primal
feasibility, dual feasibility and a duality gap at machine precision --- the
solver's own optimality certificate --- and a run in which any of the three
fails produces no bound.

\paragraph{What the certificate does and does not license.} It bounds the
distance to the best schedule \emph{of this problem}: the first-order
displacement along a fixed reference trajectory, on a fixed accumulation grid,
over a discrete degree set. It says nothing about the nonlinear trajectory,
which Section~\ref{sec:campaign-fixedpoint} tests separately.

\subsection{What the certificate cannot settle, and what does}
\label{sec:algorithm-exhaustive}

The bound above is limited by the relaxation and not by the effort spent
solving it. Solved to convergence with away steps, the relaxed optimum sits at
\ph{value} of the true integer optimum on instances small enough to check, and
the shortfall does not shrink with the number of decision intervals: it is the
price of relaxing an integer schedule to a convex mixture, and no solver
recovers it. A threshold on $g_E$ would therefore be testing how tight the
relaxation happens to be on that instance, which is a property of the
relaxation rather than of the schedule.

What settles the question is enumeration. On instances with
$K_{\mathrm{dec}}\le\ph{n}$ decision intervals and $\lvert\mathcal N\rvert=3$
candidates, every feasible schedule is evaluated and the descent is compared
against the true optimum rather than against a bound. Over the panel of
\ph{n} such instances the descent returned the exact optimum in \ph{n} of
them, with a worst-case ratio of \ph{value}.

This establishes optimality \emph{for the instances enumerated}, and nothing
more. It says nothing about $K_{\mathrm{dec}}=\ph{n}$ beyond the ordinary
inductive weight of a solver that has not been caught suboptimal where it could
be checked --- and on unstructured random instances of the same size it
\emph{is} caught, landing \ph{pct} above the enumerated optimum in the worst
case. Both facts are reported.

We therefore do not call the full-scale quantity an oracle. It is the
\Asign\ \emph{trajectory-aware attainability benchmark}: a schedule that is
attainable under the stated information and budget, whose distance from the
constrained optimum is measured where measurement is possible and bounded, when
the bound is informative, by
Section~\ref{sec:algorithm-certificate}. Ratios taken against it are read as
conservative, in the same sense the previous paper's force-level Lagrangian
benchmark was. Where the word \emph{oracle} appears it refers to information
access --- an \emph{oracle-information} schedule, one allowed to read the
reference field --- and never to optimality.

\subsection{Cost of the solve}

\ph{one paragraph} Table build per orbit; sweep count to convergence;
Frank--Wolfe iterations to a useful gap; memory of the vector table at the
converged accumulation grid. None of this is charged to the flight budget ---
the benchmark is not flown --- but it decides what is feasible to run across a
campaign, and the controller of Section~\ref{sec:controller} inherits a
reduced form of it, which \emph{is} charged. \wpref{WP1, WP3, WP7}
